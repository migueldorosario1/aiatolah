In a short, charming video released by Google DeepMind’s robotics team, the humanoid robot Apollo sits down for a casual interview — microphone and all — to reflect on its experience mastering everyday tasks powered by Gemini Robotics 2. The exchange is equal parts playful and profoundly revealing: beneath the gentle Hollywood joke lies a major leap in how AI gives physical robots the common sense, dexterity, and teamwork needed to navigate the messy real world.

Apollo, built by Apptronik, is one of the first humanoids to run Gemini Robotics 2, Google’s newest vision-language-action model tailored for embodied AI. In the clip, the robot candidly dissects three challenges designed to stress‑test its abilities. “Tying up a trash bag is quite complex,” Apollo admits about the tricky kitchen chore. This isn’t just a grab‑and‑lift task — it requires delicate manipulation of a floppy, unpredictable object, a skill that has historically confounded even the most advanced robotic hands. The Saturday sports challenge, meanwhile, tested whole‑body control and balance. For a bipedal machine, maintaining equilibrium while twisting, reaching, or kicking is a control nightmare, yet Gemini Robotics 2 allows Apollo to coordinate its joints in fluid motion, approaching human‑like athleticism. The messy garage challenge proved the most cooperative: Apollo worked alongside a second robot, Duo, to find a “Genkit” (a tool or gadget), close it, and neatly place it back into a bin. That seamless two‑robot collaboration is a quiet milestone — it shows that Gemini’s AI can understand not just the physical world but also shared goals, handing off tasks like a digital co‑worker.

Under the hood, Gemini Robotics 2 represents a fusion of Google’s best large language models with spatial and physical reasoning. Instead of coding thousands of rigid motion scripts, the system takes a high‑level instruction — “tie up the trash bag” or “put the Genkit back” — and translates it into a sequence of coordinated actions on the fly. This is the same philosophy that powers chatbots that can write poetry, now extended into the realm of touch and torque. Apollo’s silky dialogue in the video even suggests that the robot can reflect on its own performance, a hint at the kind of self‑awareness needed for lifelong learning in unstructured homes and factories.

To appreciate just how significant this is, it helps to zoom out and look at the global race toward intelligent robots — especially the fierce competition coming from China. While Apollo was chatting with a boom mic, Chinese labs have been closing the gap at astonishing speed. 

On the software side, reasoning models like DeepSeek have redefined what’s possible, matching top Western AIs at a fraction of the training cost. Alibaba’s Qwen family and Moonshot AI’s Kimi are pushing the boundary of long‑context understanding, meaning a robot could theoretically reason about an entire day’s worth of instructions without losing the plot. These language models are already being plugged into Chinese humanoids: Unitree’s H1 robot, for example, can dance and run using its own AI engine, and Xiaomi’s CyberOne is learning to grip fragile objects through similar vision‑language pipelines.

On the hardware front, China’s semiconductor push is quietly laying the foundation for cheap, powerful robot brains. Huawei’s Ascend 910B chips and the upcoming 910C are designed to serve massive AI models locally, reducing the need for cloud dependency — an architecture tailor‑made for robots that must react in real time. Companies like Fourier Intelligence and UBTECH are mass‑producing humanoids at a scale that is driving down costs, much like what happened with smartphones a decade ago. So when Apollo jokes about a future in Hollywood, the real race is actually happening in factory workshops and living rooms: who can build a robot that ties a trash bag or clears a messy garage without breaking a sweat — and do it affordably for millions of users.

The Apollo interview isn’t just a cute demo. It’s a snapshot of a world where AI leaps out of our phones and into bodies that share our physical space. Gemini Robotics 2 gives Apollo the ability to learn once and act anywhere, a transferable intelligence that echoes the same “generalist” ambition we see in DeepSeek’s reasoning or Qwen’s multimodal grasp. And as China’s own robots — powered by domestic chips and homegrown foundation models — start to close the dexterity gap, what we’re seeing is the start of a truly global push to make helpful, general‑purpose robots as common as laptops. Apollo may want to focus on daily tasks, not stardom, but for now, both it and its Chinese rivals are stealing the show.
